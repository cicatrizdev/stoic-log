<Epigraph source="Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, 91">
  The unexpected weighs heaviest. What has been foreseen wounds less when it
  arrives.
</Epigraph>

Seneca wrote that letter after a fire consumed Lyon in a single night. An entire city, and no rollback plan. His advice to his shaken friend was not "this will never happen again" — it was the exercise the Stoics called *premeditatio malorum*: walking through everything that can go wrong, in advance and with open eyes.

The software industry rediscovered this exercise twice and gave it two names. When we do it after the disaster, we call it a **post-mortem**. When we do it before — rarely — we call it a **pre-mortem**.

## Rehearsing failure is not pessimism

The classic objection to *premeditatio* has been the same for two millennia: "doesn't dwelling on the worst attract the worst?" Seneca would answer that the alternative is worse: being surprised. The exercise is not anxious rumination — it is visiting the scenario precisely, once, so it loses its power to shock.

Engineering has one advantage over life: here, rehearsing the failure *changes its probability*. When a team asks "how could this launch fail?" before the launch, it is not just preparing emotionally — it is finding the missing index, the optimistic timeout, the dependency without a fallback.

## The pre-mortem ritual

The format I use is stolen half from Gary Klein, half from Seneca:

1. **Declare the disaster as fact.** Not "what could go wrong?", but "it is six months from now and the project failed. What happened?" The change of tense disarms institutional optimism — nobody has to defend the plan, because the plan is already dead.
2. **Everyone writes alone.** Five minutes, private list. The senior who speaks first contaminates the whole room.
3. **Sort by embarrassment, not probability.** The most useful question is: *which of these causes would embarrass us most for not having foreseen it?* That one almost always deserves a test today.

<Callout label="in practice">
  Chaos engineering is premeditatio malorum with a budget: killing the pod on
  purpose is rehearsing loss in a controlled environment, exactly as Seneca
  recommended living a few days a month as a poor man — not to suffer, but to
  discover that the feared scenario is manageable.
</Callout>

## Blameless is a Stoic word

The blameless post-mortem Google popularized is, at bottom, a philosophical position: the engineer who pressed the button was operating a system, and it was the whole system that produced the outcome. Epictetus would say the same in other words — judging a person by what was not in their control is aiming the judgment at the wrong target.

A good post-mortem reads the past the way *premeditatio* reads the future: no drama, no blame, with the single question that matters — *what does this scenario teach us to prepare?*

The fire of Lyon was not prevented. The next one, perhaps.
