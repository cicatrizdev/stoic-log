<Epigraph source="Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, 2.1">
  Say to yourself at daybreak: today I shall meet the meddler, the ungrateful,
  the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious, the unsocial.
</Epigraph>

The kickoff opens with a vision slide, an optimistic timeline and the ritual line: *"if we align well at the start, the rest will flow"*. Nobody opens the meeting with *"six months from now this project will have failed — why?"*. It would sound in bad taste. It is exactly the exercise.

In the previous essay on this trail, *premeditatio malorum* showed up after the fire — the post-mortem as a rehearsal of loss already spent. Here the tense changes: failure is declared **before** the first line, while institutional optimism is still intact and, for that reason, more dangerous.

## Kickoff is peak blindness

Marcus Aurelius did not wait for the bad day to rehearse the bad day. The daybreak formula exists because morning is when the fantasy of control is freshest — and the fantasy is the enemy. A project kickoff is corporate morning: clean backlog, smiling stakeholders, nobody has yet discovered the dependency that "never goes down."

The industry ritualizes the opposite. Roadmaps only show the happy path. Estimates only admit delay gift-wrapped as "residual risk." The engineer who asks out loud *what if the partner API vanishes in month three?* is read as bad energy, not as the only adult in the room. The market selects for performative optimism — then acts surprised when the post-mortem lists what nobody wanted to hear on day zero.

Premeditatio at kickoff is not slide pessimism. It is refusing the lie that the plan deserves emotional protection. Dead plans confess more than living ones: the previous essay on this trail already showed what people will admit about a corpse. Use that while the corpse is still hypothetical — it costs less.

## A kill criterion is a deliverable, not a mood

What most kickoffs lack is not a colorful risk brainstorm. It is a **kill criterion**: the objective condition under which the team stops, pivots or kills the project without drama. Without it, *premeditatio* becomes a group venting session and failure becomes a zombie — alive in Jira, dead in practice, fed by "just one more month" sprints.

Writing the criterion on day one is Stoicism applied to a calendar. You do not control whether the market shifts, the partner slips, the scope bloats. You control the sentence, signed before attachment: *if X has not happened by Y, we shut it down*. That belongs on the first list. The hope that "it will take off" belongs on the second — and it does not pay for infra.

<Callout label="in practice">
  At kickoff, reserve fifteen mandatory minutes under the title "the project
  already failed." Everyone writes three causes alone. Then, for each cause
  that survives the embarrassment filter, demand **one** action for today: a
  feature flag, a fallback contract, a kill criterion in the RFC, an alert
  that fires before the customer does. No action, and the cause is an
  anecdote. Anecdote is not premeditatio — it is a risk slide nobody rereads.
</Callout>

## The twin almost nobody schedules

A post-mortem without a pre-mortem is the philosophy of people who only study the fire after the ashes. The reverse also limps: rehearse failure at kickoff and never look back when it arrives. The two tenses of *premeditatio* feed each other — the previous essay read the past as training; this one schedules the future as fact.

The useful question on day zero is not "will we make it?". It is: *when this fails in the most embarrassing way, what will we already have prepared?* A feature flag is not cynicism. A documented rollback is not a lack of faith. A kill criterion is not defeatism. They are engineering's version of Marcus Aurelius at daybreak: name the hard thing **before**, so it arrives without the right to surprise you.

The next kickoff will open with the vision slide. That is not up to you. Whoever asks, out loud, why the project already failed — that is.
