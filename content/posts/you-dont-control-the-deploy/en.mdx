<Epigraph source="Epictetus, Enchiridion, 1">
  Some things are within our power, and some are not.
</Epigraph>

It's 6:47pm on a Friday. The deploy went out at 6:30 — approved, tested, covered. Seventeen minutes later the dashboard goes red, the incident channel wakes up, and someone types the sentence every developer knows: *"did anyone change anything?"*

In that moment there are two possible people inside you. One spends the next twenty minutes negotiating with reality: *it wasn't supposed to fail, QA passed, it was approved in review, why today of all days.* The other one opens the runbook.

The difference between them is not technical. It is the oldest question in Stoic philosophy: **what, exactly, is up to you?**

## The two lists

Epictetus opens the *Enchiridion* with a division so simple it feels brutal. On one side, what is yours: judgment, impulse, desire, aversion — in modern terms, your attention, your response, your process. On the other, everything else: body, reputation, title. The roadmap. The market. The deploy that already shipped.

Notice the detail almost everyone gets wrong: **the ongoing incident is on the second list.** Once the package reached production, the outage is no longer something you control — it is something that happened. What remains under your control is smaller and more precise:

- Methodical diagnosis instead of anxious guessing.
- A calm rollback instead of a heroic rushed hotfix.
- The tone of your message in the incident channel.
- What you learn and write down afterwards.

```bash
# What is up to you fits in a terminal:
git revert --no-edit a1b2c3d
git push origin main
# The rest — who noticed, who's asking, what they'll say — does not.
```

## Control is not indifference

The lazy reading of Stoicism stops here: "so nothing matters, I don't control it anyway." It is the opposite. Epictetus was obsessed with responsibility — just the *right* responsibility.

You don't control whether the Friday deploy breaks. You control the test you wrote beforehand, the feature flag that let you switch it off without a redeploy, the alert that fired in two minutes instead of ten. The entire preparation lived on the first list; you exercised it, or didn't, long before 6:47pm.

<Callout label="in practice">
  Before your next risky deploy, actually write the two lists. On the first:
  what you can do **now** (tests, rollback plan, who to warn). On the second:
  everything you are tempted to ruminate about (your manager's reaction, the
  timing, luck). Work the first list to the end. Consciously refuse every item
  on the second.
</Callout>

## The Stoic developer in the war room

Marcus Aurelius ran Rome through a plague and two wars while writing himself nightly reminders about not losing his head. An incident war room is the scaled-down version of the same exercise: an environment that punishes panic and rewards whoever can separate, in real time, what happened from what to do next.

The irony is that the engineer who accepts not controlling the outage responds better to the outage. Whoever spends energy fighting the fact consumes exactly the resource — attention — that diagnosis requires.

The Friday deploy will break again someday. That is not up to you. The person at the keyboard when it happens — that one is.
