<Epigraph source="Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, 3.4">
  Do not waste the remainder of your life in thoughts about others, when you
  are not thinking with reference to some aspect of the common good.
</Epigraph>

Every week a framework is announced as the end of the previous one — usually by the creator of the next one. And every week thousands of developers feel the same small tightness in the chest: *am I falling behind?*

That tightness feels like information. It is not. It is the business model.

## The feed is a slot machine for professional anxiety

The mechanism has a name and decades of research behind it: variable reward. Sometimes the post teaches you something; sometimes it just scares you. That alternation — not the content — is what makes you pull the lever again. Vegas calls it a slot machine. We call it "keeping up".

Notice the dominant literary genre: *"X is dead."* It is not analysis; it is a format — and it pays well, because fear engages harder than curiosity. The influencer who calms you down loses to the one who terrifies you, so the market selects for terror. "Keeping up" with that stream is not hard; it is **unwinnable by design**, like any race where the finish line moves.

I say this as someone who teaches technology for a living — that is, as part of the problem whenever I get sloppy. The good news nobody posts: almost nothing needs to be learned in launch week. What is important is still important the following week — that is practically the definition of important.

## Marcus and other people's opinions

Marcus Aurelius was the most-watched man in the world: every gesture debated by the Senate, every decision judged by an empire. His response was to write, at night, an entire manual on **not renting out his own attention** — the passage in the epigraph continues with a list of what he refused to do: imagining what so-and-so is doing, saying, plotting. Rome had gossip; it did not have threads.

The feed is that at industrial scale: thinking about others as a full-time occupation. What someone shipped, what someone else thinks of what they shipped, what you are supposed to think of both. Epictetus would file all of it on the second list without getting up: other people's opinions about other people's stacks are the purest example of what is not up to you.

What is up to you: what you study with your whole attention, today.

<Callout label="in practice">
  Three questions before learning any new technology, in this order:
  **(1)** does it solve a problem I have — or one I was told to have?
  **(2)** will it exist in five years? (if the answer is "impossible to know",
  treat it as "no" until further notice) **(3)** am I curious, or am I
  afraid? Curiosity belongs to Epictetus' first list and produces deep study.
  Fear belongs to the second and produces forty open tabs.
</Callout>

## Depth compounds; novelty doesn't

The career math is less glamorous than the feed suggests. Hype knowledge has the shelf life of yogurt: a summary of the season's framework is worth nothing in three years — or worth less than nothing, as the dated reference in the middle of your interview. Depth ages in reverse. Whoever *actually* understands how the runtime works, how HTTP works, how state propagates through a UI, can debug any framework of any season — including the ones not yet released, because they will be built from the same parts.

Nobody was ever promoted for having an opinion on every framework. Plenty of people were for mastering one.

The Stoics had a word for the training that chooses depth over dispersion: *askesis*. It is not abstinence from novelty — it is the discipline of deciding **yourself** what deserves your attention, instead of letting an ad-auction algorithm decide for you.

The hype will keep shouting; that is not up to you. Your fear of falling behind is someone else's business model. Your depth is yours.
